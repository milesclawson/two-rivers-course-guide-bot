                College Credit Opportunities
A Parent & Student Guide to AP and Concurrent Enrollment
Why should you consider taking classes with college-level content and expectations in high school? To
help prepare for the academic demands of higher education! Choosing challenging courses in high
school can make a huge impact on your path to college.

This overview explains two main ways students can earn college credit early: Advanced Placement (AP)
and Concurrent Enrollment (CE) or College in the Schools (CIS) at the U of MN: These courses and the
credits are free to students (with the exception of optional additional certifications).

Success in either program will strengthen a college application by demonstrating a student's ability to
handle challenging coursework. Students are encouraged to choose courses that are of interest to
them and that are manageable with the rest of their course load and activities schedule.

Important Caveat/Note 1: The danger of Scholarship Loss.

Degree Requirements: Earning too much credit might lead to a student having to have a major at 60
credits, which is a critical planning detail related to degree progress.

The Academic Experience: Workload and Prerequisites
Both AP and CE courses involve a significantly heavier workload than standard high school classes.

One of the most important early indications of whether or not your student is ready for a workload
associated with AP and CE courses is their executive functioning skills. Planning, organizing and time
management are as critical as interest and ability.

Workload Differences
The general expectation for any college-level course—whether it's an AP class, a CE course, or a PSEO
course—is significantly higher than a regular high school class.

Commonly, college courses (AP, CE or PSEO) follow the 1:2 ratio: For every class period, a student should
expect up to 1-2 hours outside of class on homework, reading, and studying. This will vary depending on
the course, instructor, as well as other factors.

Admissions Value

    ●​ AP Courses: Admissions offices view success in AP courses as an excellent way to demonstrate
       standardized rigor and subject mastery against a nationally recognized benchmark.
    ●​ CE Courses: Taking CE courses is valued as an excellent way to show concrete college readiness
       by providing an official college transcript with an actual college GPA.

Transferring the Credit

No credit transfer is guaranteed. Colleges have the final say, so you should always check the college’s
website or registrar’s office before enrolling in a course.

    ●​ Transferability of AP Credit: AP credits are determined by the receiving institution. With national
       recognition they are received equally across state lines. Each institution will report which AP
       Tests they offer credit for and to what degree.
    ●​ Transferability of CE Credit: CE credit is guaranteed by the partner college upon passing. It
       transfers most easily within a state's public system and articulated agreements those institutions
       have. Other receiving institutions may audit the course to determine transferability.

Transfer Outcomes: Not All Credit is Created Equal

    1.​ Direct Equivalent: Credit fulfills a specific required course (e.g., AP Calculus AB).
    2.​ Elective Credit: Credit counts toward total degree hours but doesn't fulfill a specific requirement.
    3.​ No Credit: The college may only use the course for placement or not accept it at all.

What is the process for transferring CE credits?

To ensure your concurrent enrollment (or dual enrollment) credits transfer smoothly to your future
college, you must act as the bridge between the high school and the college that awarded the credit.
High school transcripts often list these classes, but they are not sufficient for transferring college credit.

Follow this step-by-step process to secure your credits:

Verify Equivalency Early

Before you even graduate, check how your current credits will be viewed by your target university.

    ●​ Use Transferology: This is a nationwide network where you can enter your current college
       courses to see how they "match" at thousands of other universities.
    ●​ Check the Transfer Matrix: Most universities have a Transfer Equivalency Table on their
       admissions website. This tells you exactly what course (e.g., English 101) your concurrent class
       will count as at their institution.
    ●​ Keep Your Syllabi: Save a PDF copy of every course syllabus. If a college doesn't automatically
       recognize a course, you can "petition" for credit by showing them the syllabus to prove the
       course's rigor and content.
                                                                                                              7
Identify the Credit-Granting Institution
Even if you took the class at your high school, the credit is technically held by a partnering college
(e.g., a local community college or state university).

    ●​ Ask your teacher: "Which college is officially granting credit for this course?"
    ●​ Multiple schools: If you took Math through one college and Spanish through another, you will
       have two different college transcripts to manage.

3. Request Official College Transcripts
This is the most critical step. Your future university will only award credit once they receive an official
transcript directly from the college that hosted the course.

    ●​ Wait for Final Grades: Do not request the transcript until your final semester grades are posted
       (usually mid-to-late June).
    ●​ Use the Registrar’s Portal: Go to the college's website and look for “Transcript Request”. Most
       colleges use services like Parchment or National Student Clearinghouse.
    ●​ Send Official Only: Have the college send the transcript directly to your future university’s
       Admissions or Registrar's Office. A student copy or a PDF you downloaded yourself is considered
       unofficial and won't be accepted for credit.

4. Confirm Receipt and Evaluation
After you send the transcripts, the work isn't done until you see the credits on your new degree plan.

    ●​ The 45-Day Rule: It can take 4–8 weeks for a university to process and evaluate transfer credits.
    ●​ Check your Student Portal: Look for a "Transfer Credit Report" or "Academic Requirements" page
       in your new college’s online system.
    ●​ Meet with an Advisor: During your first-year orientation or registration, bring an unofficial copy
       of your college transcript. If the credits haven't been posted yet, the advisor can often manually
       clear you to skip "Intro" classes so you don't take the same course twice.

Important Reminder: If you are attending a private or out-of-state university, they may be pickier
about credits. If they deny a credit, ask for a "re-evaluation" and provide the syllabus you saved in
Step 1.

Hidden Disadvantages

    ●​ Impact on College GPA (CE): The grade earned in a CE course is permanently on a college
       transcript and starts the student's college GPA. A poor grade can negatively impact future
       academic standing and scholarship eligibility.
    ●​ Scholarship Loss: Some universities offer generous merit scholarships only to first-time, full-time
       freshmen with less than a specified number of transfer credits (e.g., less than 12–15 credits).
       Earning too many credits might disqualify a student. Always check the scholarship terms.
    ●​ Repeated Coursework: The student may repeat a course they already earned credit for because
       the college insists the student has a stronger foundation or the student wants a stronger
       foundation.

Hidden Advantages:

Taking AP or CE courses doesn't directly raise your score, but it significantly improves the cognitive skills
and content mastery that form the foundation of success on these standardized tests.

Deeper Content Knowledge

Both AP and CE courses cover material with a college-level depth and pace, which extends well beyond
the typical high school curriculum and aligns closely with the academic rigor measured on the ACT and
PSAT.

    ●​ Expanded Knowledge Base: Advanced coursework in subjects like AP Calculus, CE College
        Algebra, AP U.S. History, or AP English Language and Composition forces you to master concepts
        that are frequently tested in the higher-difficulty questions on the ACT and PSAT. For example,
        advanced math coursework covers functions and data analysis that are essential for the ACT
        Math section.

    ●​ Science Reasoning Practice: AP Science courses (like Biology, Chemistry, or Physics) emphasize
        analyzing complex data, interpreting graphs, and evaluating scientific experiments—skills directly
        tested in the ACTScience section.

Enhanced Critical Thinking and Reading Skills

The nature of college-level work is to challenge students to think critically, a skill which is the core of the
English and Reading sections of both tests.

    ●​ Advanced Reading Comprehension: AP and CE courses require students to read dense, lengthy,
        and complex non-fiction texts (historical documents, scientific studies, literary criticism). This
        practice directly prepares you for the challenge of quickly and accurately comprehending the
        dense passages on the PSAT and ACT Reading sections.
    ●​ Logical Argumentation: Coursework in AP English or History involves writing complex,
        evidence-based arguments. This strengthens your ability to identify the logical structure of
        arguments and correct rhetorical flaws, which is key to the PSAT/ACT English/Writing sections.

Improved Academic Discipline

The demanding nature of these courses cultivates essential habits that are crucial for performing under
the pressure of a timed, standardized test.

    ●​ Time Management and Pacing: Managing the heavy workload, challenging assignments, and
        high expectations of an AP or CE course builds discipline and efficiency. This translates into
        better pacing and focus when taking a long standardized test.
    ●​ Stamina for Testing: The courses themselves often have significant final exams (AP Exams or
        comprehensive CEfinal exams) that require sustained focus over several hours. This practice
        builds the mental stamina needed to perform consistently throughout the multi-section ACT or
        PSAT.

Finding the Right Fit
There is no single better choice—it depends on the student and their intended college.

    ●​ Students who thrive in a structured, test-based environment may prefer AP.
    ●​ Those who desire flexibility and independence may prefer Concurrent Enrollment (CE). CE is
       often preferred by students who want the college credit rigor while remaining fully integrated in
       the high school community .

Social and Transition Concerns

Parents and students need to understand the social consequences of skipping freshman year, including
the risk of missing out on living on campus and missing out on freshman experiences.

    ●​ Age and Peer Group: Being 17 or 18 and attending college after high school graduation with
       upperclassmen, could be a social consideration for students who earn credits that move them to
       the category of sophomore or junior at the college.
    ●​ High School Balance: There is also the potential for missing outside activities in High School due
       to the heavy college-level workload.

How can I help my student decide?
Using the structured set of prompts and statements below, walk through the decision with your student,
as well as for yourself. This can help you understand what your goal(s) are for deciding which direction
to take.

Academic Goals and College Planning
   1.​ You should begin by asking: Which specific colleges (or types of colleges: in-state public,
       competitive private, out-of-state) are currently on your list? This is vital because AP credit (based
       on a high exam score) is generally more widely accepted by highly selective and out-of-state
       universities. Conversely, CE credit is often the most reliable for in-state public universities or the
       specific partner college.
   2.​ Next, clarify your purpose by asking: What major or field of study are you currently considering?
       How would this course help fulfill that major's requirements? AP courses typically cover broad
       General Education requirements, while CE courses, which perfectly match a college's own
       curriculum, might be better for fulfilling a Specific Major course sequence. Finally, identify your
       main motivation:
   3.​ Is your primary goal to save money on college tuition, gain admission to a competitive school, or
       skip introductory classes? While AP is highly valued in the admissions process as a signal of
       academic rigor, CE offers a more guaranteed way to earn credit (upon passing the course), which
       is the primary financial benefit.

Learning Style and Workload Management: The next set of questions addresses your academic
preferences and how you handle pressure and workload.

    1.​ The single most important factor here is grading: Are you a strong test-taker who can perform
        well on one comprehensive exam, or do you prefer your final grade to reflect year-long
        performance (homework, quizzes, papers)? This highlights the core difference: AP credit is

        determined entirely by one high-stakes exam in May, while CE credit is earned through the final
        course grade (typically a C or better). In terms of workload, both are rigorous, but you must ask:
        How prepared are you to dedicate an average of 8-10+ hours of outside-of-class time each week
        to this one course? CE courses are often semester-long, meaning they compress the same
        amount of college material into half the time of a year-long AP course, requiring an even faster
    2.​ Consider the environment: Do you prefer a classroom environment led by a high school teacher,
        or one that may be taught by a college professor with college-age students? AP is taught by an
        AP-trained high school teacher, but CE offers a more authentic college experience, especially if
        you take it on a college campus with a college professor.

Risk Tolerance and Transcript Impact: Understanding the long-term impact on your permanent
academic record is crucial, especially regarding risk.
    1.​ You must confront the worst-case scenario: If you were to struggle in this course and earn a 'D'

    2.​ In contrast, a final grade in a CE course, even a failing one, goes on your permanent college

    3.​ Financially, ask: How sensitive are you to course fees? Are you willing to pay a flat exam fee, or a
        potentially higher per-credit tuition cost? AP involves a standardized exam fee (around $100),

### Condensed 2-page version (same topic) adds this note on PSEO
PSEO (Postsecondary Enrollment Options) is a Minnesota state-funded program, distinct from AP and CE. PSEO students leave the high school campus to take classes at a college or university campus or online alongside full-time college students, always taught by college faculty. PSEO is tuition-free, and the state typically covers required textbooks and fees. It offers a broader selection of courses but requires significant schedule rearrangement and travel, which can make it hard for students heavily involved in high school activities. The 2-page version also says students with a 504/IEP work with the partnering institution to determine accommodations, and that a QR code on the handout links to all college-credit course offerings at Two Rivers.

## SOURCE C: TriDistrict Career & College Readiness Initiative (careercollegeready.org; fetched 2026-11-12)

What it is: Website of the TriDistrict Career and College Readiness Initiative, which runs TriDistrict CAPS (Center for Advanced Professional Studies), a partnership of the Inver Grove Heights, South St. Paul, and West St. Paul-Mendota Heights-Eagan districts. Participating high schools: Two Rivers, Simley, South St. Paul.
- Goal: help students discover a career path and make strategic choices about education after high school.
- CAPS courses are profession-based learning developed with local business partners, within the national CAPS Network. Students learn on-site at local businesses and are mentored by employers; they receive high school and college credit (the site doesn't say how many).
- Eligibility (per site): TriDistrict juniors and seniors on track to graduate with their class, with interest in the industry, and willing to commit to a higher level of accountability in attendance, behavior and flexibility. The Two Rivers planning guide lists these courses for grades 11-12 (e.g. TriDistrict CAPS Healthcare & Medicine 4013/4014).
- Format: year-long. First half: learning from people working in the field. Second semester: students are at internship sites for about 80% of the class.
- Programs listed on the enrollment page: Healthcare; Transportation & Skilled Trades; Business & Entrepreneurship; Information Technology/Computer Science; Natural Resources, Sustainability & Food Systems. Each high school also has a CAPS-like Education program (classroom plus internship, concurrent enrollment credit possible, no separate application).
- How to apply: (1) register for the course at your home high school following the counselors' registration timeline; (2) submit the online application (only online applications accepted; about 15-20 minutes; includes a recommendation from a teacher, coach, counselor or administrator, about 5 minutes for the recommender); (3) review the Student & Family Program Guide before applying (applying means agreeing to its rules). Each high school has roughly 10-15 seats per program, and the process is expected to be competitive.
- Not stated on the site: a specific deadline, transportation, cost, and scheduling details. (The Two Rivers planning guide notes the Healthcare course meets periods 5-7 at the South St. Paul Community Learning Center.)
- Contact: home-school counselor first; program director Ben Kusch, bkusch@tridistrictce.org, 612-267-2760 (as listed on the site).
- Caveats: the 2026-27 application post was dated Dec 17, 2025, but some pages still show older dates (2018-19 program strands, "fall of 2024"). Treat course names and details as needing counselor confirmation.
- Site pages: careercollegeready.org (home), /home/about/, /tridistrict-programs/, /home/courses/, /home/courses/enrollment/, /employers/.
